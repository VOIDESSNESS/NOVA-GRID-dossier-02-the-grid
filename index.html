01 — Architecture Overview

Five Layers.
One Intelligent System.

The Nova Grid architecture is not a renovation of what exists. It is a greenfield intelligent grid — five tightly integrated layers from generation to consumer, governed end-to-end by the NEXUS AI system.

The grid is conceived as a living system — not a static infrastructure. Every component communicates with every other component through a unified data fabric. Every panel, every turbine, every transformer, every smart terminal feeds real-time telemetry to NEXUS. Every customer action updates the grid's demand model. Every fault triggers an automated response before a human operator even opens their screen.

02 — Generation Strategy

Solar First.
Backed by Gas.
Evolved to Green.

Nigeria sits in one of the most solar-rich regions on earth. Exploiting this is not idealism — it is the most economically rational generation strategy for a country with this geography and this crisis.

Nigeria's solar irradiance ranges from 5.5 to 7.0 kWh/m²/day across the country — among the highest in the world. The northern states receive the most intense solar radiation. For context, Germany — Europe's solar power leader — averages 3.0–3.5 kWh/m²/day. Nigeria has roughly twice Germany's solar potential and a fraction of its solar infrastructure.
The strategy is explicit: solar utility farms form the backbone of Phase I generation. Natural gas provides firm baseload and peaking power — critical for maintaining grid frequency stability when solar generation drops at night or during Harmattan dust periods. Existing hydro is upgraded and optimised. Wind is deployed where geography supports it. Battery storage bridges supply gaps across all sources.

Nigeria's northern belt — particularly Sokoto, Kebbi, Zamfara, Kano, Katsina, and Borno states — receives solar irradiance equivalent to the world's most productive solar regions. A 10,000 MW solar array in the Sokoto-Kebbi corridor alone would match the entire current installed capacity of Nigeria's national grid. This is not a distant possibility. It is an engineering project that can begin in 2026.

Why Not 100% Renewable Immediately?

The temptation is to design a fully renewable grid from day one. The engineering and grid stability arguments against this are sound: solar generates nothing at night, and Nigeria's battery storage supply chain cannot yet support a fully renewable grid at national scale within a 6-year Phase I window.

Natural gas bridges this gap. Nigeria has some of the world's largest natural gas reserves — the most underutilised energy asset in the country. Repurposing gas for grid power (rather than flaring it) while solar infrastructure is built is both pragmatic and a significant environmental improvement over the current diesel generator economy.

The Path to 100% Clean by 2050

Phase III (2040–2050) targets a grid that is over 90% renewable — solar, wind, hydro, and green hydrogen (produced using surplus solar energy) providing firm power without fossil fuels.

This is achievable because: (1) battery storage costs are falling 15–20% annually; (2) green hydrogen technology will mature significantly by 2035; (3) expanded hydro capacity at the Benue Trough and Cross River systems provides reliable baseload renewable energy throughout Phase II.

20,000 MW of Panels

04 — Wind Energy

Works for Nigeria

Nigeria's wind resources are concentrated in specific zones: the Jos Plateau (1,200–1,800m elevation with consistent wind), the coastal and offshore Gulf of Guinea belt (reliable sea breezes), and the northern plains where seasonal Harmattan winds offer additional generation. 05 — Gas, Hydro & Backup Systems

The Firm Power
Foundation

Solar and wind provide cheap, clean generation — but they are variable. Natural gas and hydropower provide firm, dispatchable power: available on demand, 24/7, regardless of weather. This is the grid's stability backbone.

Natural Gas — Nigeria's Underutilised Asset

Nigeria holds the 9th largest natural gas reserves in the world — approximately 209 trillion cubic feet. The majority is currently either flared (wasted and polluting) or exported as LNG. The absurdity: Nigeria exports gas while its power plants sit idle for lack of gas supply.

Phase I dedicates 12,000 MW of combined cycle gas turbine (CCGT) capacity to firm baseload generation. These are not the ageing single-cycle turbines of NEPA — they are modern, highly efficient CCGT units operating at 55–60% thermal efficiency (vs 30% for legacy plants).

Dedicated gas pipelines — separate from the NNPCL supply lines that feed the existing broken plants — connect directly from gas fields to generation stations. NEXUS manages gas supply contracts, pipeline pressure monitoring, and automatic fallback switching to backup fuel reserves.

Hydropower — Restoring & Expanding

Nigeria's existing hydro infrastructure is vastly underperforming. Kainji (installed capacity: 760 MW, actual output: ~300–400 MW), Jebba (installed: 578 MW, actual: ~350 MW), and Shiroro (installed: 600 MW, actual: ~350 MW) are all operating below capacity due to sedimentation, ageing turbines, and poor maintenance.

Rehabilitation programme: All three major hydro plants undergo full turbine replacement, penstock repair, and SCADA integration — increasing combined hydro output from ~1,000 MW to ~1,800 MW.

New hydro sites: The Mambilla Plateau hydroelectric project (3,050 MW — one of the largest potential hydro projects on the continent, development-ready since the 1970s), Zungeru Phase II, and multiple run-of-river projects on the Benue, Cross River, and Kaduna rivers bring Phase I hydro total to 6,500 MW.

06 — Battery Storage System

The Grid's Memory:
6,000 MWh of Storage

Battery storage is the technology that turns an intermittent solar grid into a reliable one. It stores surplus daytime generation for evening peak demand, provides frequency regulation services, and acts as emergency reserve when generation falters.

Phase I deploys 6,000 MWh (megawatt-hours) of grid-scale battery energy storage systems (BESS) across 24 strategic nodes — co-located with major generation sites and large urban load centres. Each BESS installation is managed by NEXUS in real-time, charged when generation exceeds demand and discharged when demand exceeds supply.
Technology
LFP
Lithium Iron Phosphate (LFP) chemistry — chosen for superior thermal stability in Nigeria's hot climate, cycle life (4,000+ cycles), and lower fire risk compared to NMC chemistry.
Total Phase I Capacity
6,000 MWh
Distributed across 24 grid nodes. Expandable — each node designed for modular capacity addition as costs fall (projected 50% cost reduction by 2032).
Response Time
<100ms
Grid frequency response from BESS within 100 milliseconds of frequency deviation — critical for preventing cascade failures. NEXUS triggers discharge automatically.
Round-Trip Efficiency
92–95%
LFP round-trip efficiency — 92–95% of energy stored is recovered on discharge. Minimal storage loss even across daily charge/discharge cycles.
Thermal Management
Active
Active liquid cooling for all BESS installations — maintains cells within 15–35°C operating range regardless of ambient temperature. Critical in northern Nigeria's 45°C+ summers.
Operational Life
15–20 yrs
With active thermal management and NEXUS-optimised charge/discharge cycles, Phase I BESS installations are designed for 15–20 year operational life before cell replacement.
07 — Transmission Network

The 330kV Spine:
Power Moved at Scale

The transmission network is the motorway system of the grid — it moves bulk power from where it is generated (largely the north) to where it is consumed (largely the south). It must be fault-tolerant, fully monitored, and managed by NEXUS with zero single points of failure.

Nigeria's existing 330kV transmission network covers approximately 14,000 km — but much of it is ageing, overloaded, and not equipped with modern SCADA/EMS systems. Phase I involves complete instrumentation of the existing network plus construction of 8,000 km of new 330kV lines — primarily the north-to-south generation corridors from the new solar farms to southern load centres.

08 — Distribution Architecture

Last Mile:
Where the Grid
Meets the People

Distribution is where Nigeria's power has historically been lost — in ageing cables, unmetered connections, transformer theft, and billing fraud. The Nova Grid distribution layer is redesigned from first principles with built-in intelligence and zero tolerance for loss.

The distribution network spans from 33kV primary feeders through 11kV secondary networks to the 415V/240V final distribution at street and building level. Every transformer is smart-monitored. Every feeder has automated fault detection. Every connection point has a smart terminal unit. NEXUS sees everything — in real time, at every level.
Smart Distribution Transformers

Every distribution transformer (typically 500kVA–2,000kVA) is upgraded or replaced with a smart version — equipped with:

  • → Load monitoring (kW, kVAR, power factor, temperature)
  • → Phase imbalance detection and alerting
  • → Oil temperature monitoring with degradation prediction
  • → Theft detection via load vs billing reconciliation
  • → Remote tap changing for voltage regulation

Nigeria requires approximately 250,000 distribution transformers at Phase I scale. A domestic manufacturing partnership is recommended to build transformer assembly capacity in-country — creating an estimated 8,000 direct manufacturing jobs.

Automated Feeder Management

Every 33kV and 11kV feeder is equipped with automated sectionalising switches — devices that can isolate faulted segments of a feeder without cutting power to the entire circuit. When NEXUS detects a fault, the system isolates only the affected segment within 2 seconds, reroutes power via alternative feeders where available, and dispatches a maintenance team with precise fault location.

Current Nigerian practice: an entire feeder — potentially serving 50,000–200,000 customers — goes dark when one segment faults, because there is no intelligence to isolate. The fault must be manually located and repaired before power is restored. This takes hours to days.

Nova Grid target: Average restoration time from fault to full section isolation: <2 seconds automated. Average repair and full restoration: <4 hours (from current average of 18–48 hours).

10 — NEXUS: The Intelligence

NEXUS:
The Brain That
Never Sleeps

NEXUS is the artificial intelligence system that operates the entire Nova Grid infrastructure — from 330kV transmission to the smart terminal in a Lagos flat. It is not a monitoring dashboard. It is an active, decision-making system that keeps the grid alive.

NEXUS is the first answer to one of Nigeria's deepest infrastructure problems: the system has historically been managed by humans operating on incomplete information, under political pressure, and with limited technical tools. Human management has failed. NEXUS does not make political decisions. It does not accept bribes. It does not go on strike. It operates at machine speed on real-time data and makes the grid work — with humans remaining in the loop only for decisions that genuinely require human judgment.

11 — Smart Metering System

Replacing Every
Prepaid Meter
in Nigeria

Nigeria's prepaid meter is the weakest link in its power infrastructure — a physical device that can be tampered with, bypassed, falsified, or simply not installed. The Nova Grid smart terminal replaces it entirely with a system that is smarter, cheaper to operate, and impossible to defraud.

The Nova Grid Smart Terminal Unit (STU) is a hardware-software hybrid that replaces the traditional prepaid meter. The hardware is a tamper-evident, weather-rated terminal installed at the customer's connection point. The intelligence — the metering, billing, and control logic — runs in NEXUS. This means: the physical device has no exploitable firmware, no hackable display logic, and no value in theft. The meter is the system, not the box.

13 — Grid Security Architecture

Protecting the
Nation's Power

A national grid is critical infrastructure — a target for sabotage, cyberattack, and physical vandalism. The Nova Grid security architecture treats both physical and cyber threats with equal seriousness.

Cybersecurity

The operational technology (OT) network — SCADA, NEXUS, substation automation — is completely air-gapped from public internet. Physical security airgaps prevent remote intrusion into control systems. NEXUS communicates with edge devices through a dedicated private network using certificate-based mutual authentication.
